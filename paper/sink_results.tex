\newcommand{\RESULTSREDUCTION}{%
Table~\ref{tab:theory} checks Theorem~\ref{thm:cone} on every layer-level graph of every example: \NCellsTotal{} graphs in total. There are \NViolations{} violations of any bound. In the \NSinkSettings{} settings whose models place \SinkMassRange{} of attention on token~0, \PctConedRange{} of graphs are exactly coned ($\defect_0=0$). On each of these graphs, $H_1$ is empty as required by prediction C2. For comparison, $\defect_0$ and $1$D total persistence have Spearman correlation \RhoDeltaHone{} across graphs (C3); this includes the \RhoDeltaHoneNearN{} settings above \RhoDeltaHoneNearCut{} coned where both measures are nearly constant (\RhoDeltaHoneNearHOneZero{} of graphs have no $1$D bars) and thus the correlation is not weak but instead degenerate. Elsewhere it is at least \RhoDeltaHoneRest{}. Both $P_0$ and the star weight $\stw_0=(N{-}1)(1-\bar m)$ depend on length, so their raw per-layer rank correlation (median $\ge$\RhoRawMin{}) depends in part on length. Dividing both by $N-1$ gives the median per-layer rank correlation between $P_0/(N-1)$ and $1-\bar m$ at least \RhoMin{} for each sink setting (C1). Length-normalized $0$D total persistence, up to rank, is thus one minus the mean attention paid to the first token in most layers. The single worst individual layer reaches only \RhoLayerMinRange{} per setting. Figure~\ref{fig:layers} locates coning. Coning occurs in the middle of the network, where there is much attention from the sink. In none of the settings does any of the first few layers receive most attention from the sink. A chunk of later layers is sink-dominant in some models (TinyLlama, the Qwen models), and these later layers almost never cone. When this happens, it means that Theorem~\ref{thm:cone}(b) applies, allowing the length-normalized correlation to fall when $(N-1)\defect_0$ is comparable to the spread of $\stw_0$ across examples.

\paragraph{Models without a first-token sink as a contrast.} We have \NNoSinkModels{} models with essentially no attention on token~0, \NoSinkModels{}, with \NoSinkMass{} attention there. None of their graphs are coned at $s=0$, so they do not benefit from the guarantees of Theorem~\ref{thm:cone} at the first token. These two models are different. For example, while \NoSinkConedAnyMax{} of \NoSinkConedAnyMaxModel{}'s graphs have an apex at all (though these \NoSinkConedAnyMax{} have strong attention, just not on token~0, \S\ref{sec:toha}): \NoSinkConedAnyMin{} of \NoSinkConedAnyMinModel{}'s graphs are coned, with no vertex coning any of them, while \NoSinkConedAnyMax{} of \NoSinkConedAnyMaxModel{}'s graphs are coned, with strong attention on some but not all. These \NNoSinkModels{} models have $H_1$ with \NoSinkHOneNonempty{} coverage, which makes it interesting to compare how well their classifiers perform: The raw $\rho(P_0,\stw_0)$ is high (\RhoNoSinkRaw{}), but nearly all of that comes from sharing the same lengths, and the length-normalized correlation is \RhoNoSink{}. The weight, data and template of both these models are different from the sink-dominated models. So this is not a controlled intervention. Rather, the intervention here is deflation (C4, below). Sink-dominated attention is a property of the reduction, but it does not affect the pipeline itself.
}

\newcommand{\RESULTSBEYOND}{%
Note that if $0$D persistence were identical to the star, the classifier based on the two sink features would be identical to the classifier based on the two $0$D features in each layer. Table~\ref{tab:tests} (``Sink vs.\ 0D'') shows the two banks differ by \TOneRange{} AUC across the \NComp{} sink settings evaluated, and are statistically equivalent within $\pm0.015$ in \NTOneEquiv{} of them (the TOST power of this comparison is modest, so non-equivalence is not evidence of a difference). Adding $0$D to the sink bank gains \TTwoRange{}.

\paragraph{The residual lives exactly where the theory says it can.} We split layers without using labels: coned layers ($\ge90\%$ of graphs coned) and open layers ($<50\%$). Adding the coned layers' $0$D features to \textsc{Sink} changes AUC by \SplitConed{}, whereas the open layers account for \SplitOpen{} (Appendix~\ref{app:tables}). Almost everything $0$D adds beyond sink attention comes from the layers where the sink does not dominate.

\paragraph{Per head, the reduction is tighter.} Per-head sink mass and per-head $0$D are far stronger than their head-averaged versions (Table~\ref{tab:auc}), but adding per-head MST total ($P_{0,h}$) to per-head sink mass changes AUC by only \PHTopoRange{}, which is equivalent within $\pm0.015$ in \NPHTopoEquiv{} of the \NPHTopoTotal{} sink settings with per-head features.

\paragraph{The coning defect is a signal, but not a new one.} Theorem~\ref{thm:cone} identifies $\defect_0$ as the unique locus where topology may diverge from the sink. We evaluate it as a signal. Head-averaged, it is weak (AUC \DefLayerAuc{}). Per head, it is strong (\DefHeadAuc{}), with a small improvement when stacked on top of per-head sink mass (\DefHSinkRange{} AUC; 95\% interval above 0 in \DefHSinkSig{} of \DefTotal{} settings). Stacking on top of the non-topological probes yields smaller gains (\DefHNTRange{}; above 0 in \DefHNTSig{}, equivalent within $\pm0.015$ in \DefHNTEquiv{} of \DefTotal{}).

\paragraph{Deleting the sink exposes some, redundant, structure.} Deflated PH (C4) gives a modest gain of \TThreeTQA{} over \textsc{Sink} on TruthfulQA (95\% interval excludes 0 in \NDeflTQASig{} of \NDeflTQATotal{} sink settings) but less on the on-policy benchmark (\TThreeOnp{}; \NDeflOnpSig{} of \NDeflOnpTotal{}). The same holds for answer-only PH (Appendix~\ref{app:tables}). Attention graphs do contain some non-sink geometry, particularly on teacher-forced text. But this geometry is captured by non-topological probes (\S\ref{sec:detection}).
}

\newcommand{\RESULTSONEDIM}{%
Under i.i.d.\ weights, $1$D persistence is thus growing faster than linearly in the lengths relevant for us and linearly asymptotically (Proposition~\ref{prop:morse}; Figure~\ref{fig:synthetic}, left). For real attention, almost no $1$D bars exist (Table~\ref{tab:theory}) and they appear only on graphs with a non-zero coning defect. Figure~\ref{fig:synthetic} (middle) simulates this change in a controlled manner: as we increase the logit bias of the sink, random causal attention goes from looking i.i.d.-like to doing exact coning, while $H_1$ empties out in lockstep.

This resolves a common finding in practice, namely that $1$D features do not add much to $0$D. At full sample size, adding length-normalized $1$D to $0$D adds between \TFourRange{} to AUC, which makes it identical within $\pm0.015$ in \NTFourEquiv{} of \NComp{} sink settings; the exceptions are \TFourExceptions{}; of these, adding $1$D has a significant positive effect on AUC in \TFourHelps{}; the other exception has a negative point estimate but an interval that is too wide to be certain about equality. Note that the improvement seen in TruthfulQA Qwen2.5-3B is also the most sensitive result in terms of inference precision (Appendix~\ref{app:impl}). We want to emphasize that this is not because loops are generally uninformative. For instance, in a strongly sinked model, if a loop exists, there must be some token paying more attention to another token than one of those two tokens is paying to the sink, and the duration of such a loop will be limited by how much extra attention one of them pays to the other. See Figure~\ref{fig:synthetic} (right). $H_1$ can readily find a planted 6-cycle under a moderate sink ($b=6, \gamma=8$: AUC \PlantModerate{}). But a strong sink ($b=8, \gamma\le4$: AUC $\le$\PlantStrong{}) will mask it unless it is strong enough to shatter the cone. Without any sink ($b=0$) $H_1$ will also fail (AUC $\le$\PlantNone{}), since the random attention will already have plenty of background cycles. $1$D persistence is useful only in between.
}

\newcommand{\RESULTSDETECTION}{%
Table~\ref{tab:auc} places attention-graph topology among standard detectors. Three patterns hold across benchmarks.

\emph{Cheap non-topological probes dominate topology.} On TruthfulQA and the on-policy benchmark, the mean-pooled hidden-state probe (\HiddenAll{}) and per-head Lookback ratios (\LookbackAll{}) outperform all topological banks, and combined with log-probability, LLM-Check, and row statistics score \NonTopoAll{}. With this combination, adding $0$D PH changes AUC from \IZeroRange{} (equivalent within $\pm0.015$ in \NIZeroEquiv{} of the \NIZeroTotal{} settings with per-head features), and adding sink-deflated PH changes AUC from \IDeflRange{} (equivalent in \NIDeflEquiv{} of \NIZeroTotal{}). To check whether early fusion hides a small bank inside a ${\sim}2{,}000$-dimensional one, we repeat the test with late fusion. Stacking the $0$D score onto the non-topological score changes AUC by \LFZeroNTRange{} (equivalent in \LFZeroNTEquiv{} of \LFZeroNTTotal{} settings; the 95\% interval lies above 0 in \LFZeroNTSig{} of them), and stacking the deflated-PH score changes it by \LFDeflNTRange{} (equivalent in \LFDeflNTEquiv{} of \LFDeflNTTotal{}).

\emph{Whether text is teacher-forced remains an open question.} Log-probability is weak (\LogpTQA{}) on TruthfulQA, which reflects the benchmark's focus on imitative falsehoods. $0$D PH (\ZeroDTQA{}) outscores \NLogpBeatsZeroDTQA{} of \NLogpZeroDTQATotal{} models, significantly for \NLogpZeroDTQA{}. On-policy, log-probability is strong (\LogpOnp{}) and significantly beats $0$D (\ZeroDOnp{}) for \NLogpOnpSig{} of \NLogpOnpTotal{} models. Single-teacher-forced benchmarks cannot be used to draw conclusions about topology vs.\ confidence.

\emph{Resolution per-head trumps topology.} Head-averaged features are weaker than per-head features of any type, and per-head entropy lies within \PHEntRange{} AUC of per-head $0$D (Appendix~\ref{app:tables}).
}

\newcommand{\RESULTSARTIFACTS}{%
A TF-IDF model on the answer text alone attains \LexTQA{} AUC on TruthfulQA and \LexHE{} on HaluEval, but only \LexOnp{} on-policy: teacher-forced benchmarks reward recognizing how the dataset's answers were written \citep{hussain2026parallax,janiak2025illusion}. We draw no conclusions from HaluEval, where lexical separability is near perfect.
}

\newcommand{\RESULTSDISCUSSION}{%
\paragraph{What attention-graph topology measures.} In decoder-only models, Vietoris--Rips persistence of an attention graph, and all stable summaries of it, depend on one scalar per graph: the coning defect of the first token. So does TOHA's prompt--response divergence, where the prompt plays the role of the apex. Where it is zero, as in most middle layers of every sink model, $0$D total persistence equals $(N{-}1)(1-\bar m)$, a function of length and sink attention alone, and higher homology is empty. Where it is positive, Theorem~\ref{thm:cone} gives an upper bound on the deviation of topology from the sink; in our data that is mostly the first layers. Practical guidelines follow in Appendix~\ref{app:recs}.
}

\newcommand{\MISTRALAPPENDIX}{%
Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2 is the most sink-dominated model in this study, and the reduction is correspondingly tightest there. In its \texttt{[INST]} format it puts \MistralSinkMass{} of its attention on token 0 --- more than any smaller model, and \MistralConed{} of its \MistralCells{} layer graphs are exactly coned ($\defect_0=0$), with \MistralViol{} violations of any bound in Theorem~\ref{thm:cone}.

A previous check of this model's accuracy level indicated that $99.99\%$ of its $1$D features are exactly zero and that the $0$D and $0$D$+$$1$D banks are equivalent. This also indicated that near-universal coning could explain it, but did not check $\defect_0$ directly. On the graphs themselves, both halves now check out: $1$D persistence is exactly zero on \MistralHOneZero{} of them, and $\defect_0=0$ checks out on \MistralConed{}. The length-normalized rank correlation between $P_0/(N{-}1)$ and $1-\bar m$ has median \MistralRhoNorm{} over layers and never drops below \MistralRhoNormMin{}, so on this model $0$D total persistence is, up to rank, an affine function of sink attention in every layer. Per head, \MistralTohaConed{} of its graphs are exactly prompt-coned. Its median $\rho(d,\bar\pi)$ is \MistralTohaRho{}, and Proposition~\ref{prop:toha} has \MistralTohaViol{} violations.
}

\newcommand{\RESULTSTOHA}{%
We test Proposition~\ref{prop:toha} on every head of every example: \TohaCells{} head graphs from \TohaSettings{} settings (Table~\ref{tab:toha}). We then run TOHA's head-selection algorithm \citep[Alg.~1]{toha} inside our cross-validation, selecting heads on the whole training fold, which is larger than TOHA's probe set. We run it once on TOHA's score $d$ and, unchanged, on the two first-order scores the proposition names: the $\bar\pi$-score $\frac1{|R|}\sum_{u}(1-\pi_u)$ and the response sink score $1-\bar a_0=\frac1{|R|}\sum_u(1-A_{u0})$.

\emph{The identity holds on real heads.} The sandwich has \TohaViol{} violations. Our own implementation of the score $d$, so we also verify it against the authors' released code: on \NativeCells{} head graphs from \NativeSettings{} settings, our \textsc{MTop-Div} routine \citep{toha} using ripser instead of our dense Prim's algorithm, agrees to \NativeMaxDiff{}. There are \TohaConedRange{} exact prompt cones for each setting. In these head graphs, $d$ equals the $\bar\pi$-score up to float rounding (largest gap \TohaGapMax{}). We see that the median per-head rank correlation across examples between $d$ and the $\bar\pi$-score is \TohaRhoRange{}. It is \TohaRhoSinkRange{} in the sink models, but \TohaRhoSinkNoSink{} in \TohaNoSinkModels{}, which have no first-token sink: there $d$ tracks the largest prompt attention just as tightly while being, if anything, mildly anti-correlated with attention to token 0. The reduction is to max-prompt attention; that this coincides with the sink is a property of the model, not of the theorem. Shifting the sink's attention logit shifts these quantities (Appendix~\ref{app:causal}).

\emph{Topology has little effect on the first-order score.} Stacking $d$ onto the $\bar\pi$-score changes AUC by \TohaLFMaxpRange{}. Equivalent to zero within $\pm0.015$ in \TohaLFMaxpEquiv{} of \TohaLFMaxpTotal{}; the 95\% interval is above 0 in \TohaLFMaxpPos{}. Using supervised probes on all heads (TOHA's own setting for feature comparison), the $\bar\pi$-score is \TohaSupMaxpRange{} different from $d$ (equivalent in \TohaSupMaxpEquiv{} of \TohaSupMaxpTotal{}). The response sink score is \TohaSupSinkRange{} different from $d$ (equivalent in \TohaSupSinkEquiv{}). Exceptions are the models without a first-token sink (\TohaNoSinkModels{}), where the sink score is worse, and one on-policy setting which crosses the margin on the positive side. TOHA's discrete head selection is noisier: running on the $\bar\pi$-score changes AUC by \TohaMaxpRange{} (significantly higher in \TohaMaxpPos{} settings, lower in \TohaMaxpNeg{}, non-inferior at margin 0.015 in \TohaMaxpNonInf{} of \TohaMaxpTotal{}). Running on the sink score, it loses up to \TohaSinkLossSink{} AUC in the sink models (\TohaSinkLossNoSink{} in \TohaNoSinkModels{}, which have no first-token sink): it selects heads which are prompt-coned on \TohaSelConedRange{} of examples, while the sink is only the top prompt token on \TohaSelArgZeroRange{} of responses. Stacking the $0$D score on top of the non-topological score shifts the AUC by \TohaLFNTRange{}; by the above, whatever it adds is carried by the $\bar\pi$-score.

\emph{Why ``attention to $\langle s\rangle$'' looked weak.} \citet[Table~9]{toha} report that attention to the first token is a much weaker feature than their divergence. Proposition~\ref{prop:toha} identifies the quantity that $d$ actually tracks: per head, the attention of the response tokens to their most-attended prompt token. In the sink models, that token is the sink for every response token in \TohaArgZeroRange{} of head graphs. 
}

\newcommand{\CAUSALTEXT}{Proposition~\ref{prop:toha} states that TOHA should correlate more closely with the response sink score the stronger the sink. We can check this directly by intervening on the sink itself. For Qwen2.5-1.5B and TinyLlama on TruthfulQA, we add a constant $b\in\{-2,0,2,4\}$ to the first token's attention logit in every head and recompute all per-head quantities (Table~\ref{tab:causal}). The intervention does not affect the identity (no violations in any run). It affects the structure the proposition says matters. Weakening the sink ($b=-2$) reduces the fraction of prompt-coned head graphs from \CausalQwConedZero{} to \CausalQwConedLo{} (Qwen) and from \CausalTlConedZero{} to \CausalTlConedLo{} (TinyLlama), the fraction where the sink is every response token's top prompt token from \CausalQwArgZero{} to \CausalQwArgLo{} and from \CausalTlArgZero{} to \CausalTlArgLo{}, and the median rank correlation between $d$ and the response sink score from \CausalQwRhoZero{} to \CausalQwRhoLo{} and from \CausalTlRhoZero{} to \CausalTlRhoLo{}. Strengthening it ($b=4$) increases the correlation to \CausalQwRhoHi{} and \CausalTlRhoHi{}. In TinyLlama all three numbers increase monotonically with $b$. In Qwen they peak at $b=2$ and drop a little at $b=4$. TOHA's own AUC hardly changes (\CausalQwTohaRange{} and \CausalTlTohaRange{}). The gap to the sink-score version is not monotone in $b$, showing that our intervention affects TOHA's accuracy through the attention graph rather than being a dose-response.}

\newcommand{\RESULTSRECOMMENDATIONS}{%
(i) Compare a topological detector against the first-order statistic named by the reduction, and report sink attention and $\defect_0$ (or the relevant defect, such as $\defect_P$ for TOHA).
(ii) Compare topological detectors against per-head non-topological statistics (sink mass, entropy, Lookback ratios) at the same resolution, as well as against a hidden-state probe.
(iii) Evaluate on the model's own generations rather than using teacher-forced benchmarks. These mix detection and recognition of dataset authorship.
(iv) If we want to go beyond the sink, either remove the sink first (deflation) or consider only answer tokens. These reveal real non-sink geometry, but in our settings it was not new information.
}
